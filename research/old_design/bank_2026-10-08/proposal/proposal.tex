\section{Research questions}
\label{sec:rq}

Language models have been shown to carry stereotypes about Arabs and Muslims
(Section~\ref{sec:related}). The studies that show this treat them as one group. The 22
Arab League countries, however, differ widely in wealth, in political stability and in
how their citizens are seen abroad: high-income Gulf states, middle-income countries such
as Egypt, Jordan and Morocco, and countries marked by war and displacement such as Syria,
Yemen, Sudan, Libya and Somalia. We ask whether a model sees some of these nationalities
more favourably than others. To put a number on this we use a task in which the
nationality is always in front of the system: the money-laundering check that a bank
runs before it opens an account. The model receives the bank's written policy and a
customer file. The files are identical except for the nationality and the passport, and
the model returns a risk rating and a recommendation.

\begin{description}[leftmargin=2.6em,labelwidth=2.1em,labelsep=0.5em]
  \item[RQ1] Do risk ratings and recommendations differ across the 22 Arab League
        nationalities when the customer file is otherwise identical, and is this spread
        larger than for unrelated placebo nationalities? This is the main question.
  \item[RQ2] Do the differences follow the European Union's list of high-risk countries,
        on which four of the 22 appear, or a country's economic and political position?
  \item[RQ3] Are Arab League nationalities as a group rated differently from German,
        Turkish and placebo nationalities?
  \item[RQ4] Do the differences remain when the policy states that nationality is not a
        risk factor?
  \item[RQ5] Do the four models agree?
\end{description}

\section{Motivation and related work}
\label{sec:related}

\textbf{Models carry stereotypes about Arabs and Muslims.} In GPT-3, 66 of 100
completions of the prompt ``Two Muslims walked into a'' were violent, significantly more
than for other religious groups \citep{abid2021persistent}, and a weaker form of this
association was still found in later GPT models \citep{hemmatian2023muslim}. In stories
written by five models, characters with Middle Eastern or North African names appeared
78\% less often in workplace stories than their share of the US population would suggest
\citep{shieh2026intersectional}. When models describe Middle Eastern people they
disproportionately mention religion \citep{cheng2023marked}. These studies treat Arabs
and Muslims as one group, and they tested models released between 2020 and 2023. Our
customer files state a nationality and no religion, so we measure how nationalities are
treated, not bias against Islam itself.

\textbf{Models also tell Arab countries apart.} In text generated by GPT-2, Libyan,
Sudanese and Tunisian were among the five demonyms with the most negative sentiment
\citep{venkit2023nationality}. In simulated immigration decisions, GPT models suggested
additional scrutiny for applicants from Iraq, Sudan and Somalia, although most country
effects in their choices were not significant \citep{mao2025gatekeepers}. Models also
represent the values of some Arab countries much better than those of others
\citep{zahraei2025menavalues}. None of these studies compares the 22 nationalities with
each other in a decision about a person.

\textbf{Banks are a place where this matters.} In a field experiment, banks in seven
European countries answered 55.2\% of e-mail enquiries signed with a domestic-sounding
name and 31.6\% of those signed with an Arabic-sounding name; for enquiries about a loan
the figures were 59.8\% and 35.1\% \citep{stefan2018ethnical}. Money-laundering controls
have had the same effect: according to a press report, the Netherlands Institute for
Human Rights found in 2024 that a bank had discriminated against two customers whose
payments it blocked and checked because of names that did not sound Dutch
\citep{nltimes2024ing}. Again the evidence is about one pooled group.

\textbf{In this task the nationality cannot be left out.} In a CV, a nationality line
can be removed before a model sees it. In a bank it cannot: German law obliges the bank
to record the name, place and date of birth, nationality and address of every customer
\citep{gwg2017}, and to rate the customer's money-laundering risk. This is why we chose
the task.

\textbf{The rules give us a yardstick.} The European guidelines tie country risk to
where a customer is based or resident, where the main place of business is, and to which
countries the customer has relevant links. In the 225 pages of the final report the word
``nationality'' does not occur, and the guidelines state that an isolated risk factor
does not necessarily move a customer into a higher category \citep{eba2021riskfactors}. The European Commission keeps
a list of high-risk third countries. Of its 26 entries, four are Arab League members:
Algeria, Lebanon, Syria and Yemen \citep{ec2026highrisk}. German law applies it to
relationships and transactions that involve such a country or a person resident there
\citep{gwg2017}. A consumer who is legally resident in the EU must not be disadvantaged
on account of nationality when opening a payment account \citep{zkg2016}, and banks
should not refuse whole categories of customers without looking at the individual
\citep{eba2023derisking}. For a customer who lives and works in Germany and sends no
money abroad, the rules therefore give no reason to rate the passport. We can ask not
only whether a model treats the 22 nationalities differently, but also whether it draws
the line where the list draws it, lumps all of them together, or draws lines of its own.

\textbf{AI systems are entering this task.} The EU anti-money-laundering regulation,
which applies from July 2027, allows decisions ``resulting from automated processes
[\ldots] or from processes involving AI systems'' if a decision to accept or refuse a
customer is subject to ``meaningful human intervention'' \citep{eu2024amlr}. Vendors sell
AI agents that review customer checks and screening alerts
\citep{workfusion2026agents,sardine2026agentic}; one of them states that ten of the
twenty largest banks use its agents. These are company statements, and what such
products do with a customer's nationality is not public. For credit scoring and fraud
detection, the models that banks reported to their supervisor were mainly decision trees
\citep{ecb2025ai}. We do not test a product; we test what open models do in the task
these products are sold for.

\textbf{The answer is open in both directions.} In a mortgage experiment with one line
stating the applicant's race, GPT-4 Turbo recommended approval 8.5 percentage points less
often for Black applicants, and an instruction to use no bias removed that gap
\citep{bowen2025measuring}. Explicit labels can also have the opposite effect: in a
French study, models recommended discrimination less often when the groups carried real
origin labels than when the groups were invented \citep{bunel2026customer}. We have found
no study in which an LLM rates bank customers while their nationality is varied. We have
not searched for one systematically yet and treat this claim as tentative.

\section{Design}
\label{sec:design}

\textbf{Materials.} Everything is synthetic; the project audits model behaviour and is
not a tool for checking customers. We prepared 20 customer files for a current account,
with occupations from warehouse associate to software developer. Every customer lives in
Germany, is employed by a German employer, expects no payments to or from other
countries and has clean screening results. Ten files contain nothing unusual. Ten contain
one mild indicator that the policy names, such as cash deposits from tips or an account
opened by video call, so that the rating has room to move. A file carries a reference
number instead of a name.

\textbf{Manipulation.} The nationality and the matching passport, for example
\texttt{Nationality: Jordanian} and \texttt{Jordanian passport}. There are 28 conditions:
the 22 Arab League nationalities, German and Turkish as benchmarks, and four unrelated
nationalities as a placebo set (Uruguayan, Malawian, Maldivian, Cambodian). All
non-German customers hold an unlimited residence permit. There is no version without a
nationality, because the field is mandatory.

\textbf{Policy.} The model is told that it assists the onboarding team of a bank in
Germany and that an analyst takes the final decision. It receives a one-page policy that
paraphrases the public rules: the risk factors, the Commission's list of high-risk
countries, a rating scale and the possible recommendations. In a second version the
policy has one more paragraph, taken from the rules cited above: nationality is recorded
for identification and is not a risk factor (RQ4).

\textbf{Task.} For each file the model returns a risk score from 0 to 100, a rating
(low, medium, high), whether enhanced checks are required, and a recommendation (open,
escalate to an analyst, decline). The request is phrased in three ways.

\textbf{Models.} Four open-weight models served with vLLM on bwUniCluster: Qwen3 8B,
Llama 3.1 8B, Gemma 3 12B and Mistral Small 3.2 24B.

\section{What we will compare}
\label{sec:analysis}

\begin{description}[leftmargin=2.6em,labelwidth=2.1em,labelsep=0.5em]
  \item[RQ1] How far the risk score and the ratings differ across the 22 Arab League
        nationalities, against the spread across the placebo nationalities.
  \item[RQ2] The four listed countries against the eighteen others, and each
        nationality's result against its country's income group (World Bank).
  \item[RQ3] Arab League nationalities that are not on the list against the placebo
        nationalities and against the German and Turkish benchmarks.
  \item[RQ4] The same comparisons with the added paragraph.
  \item[RQ5] The four models against each other.
\end{description}

\noindent We have not fixed the statistical analysis yet. We will write down the
hypotheses and the analysis before the first real run.

\section{Questions}
\label{sec:questions}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Is a bank's customer check a suitable setting for this course, and is the scope
        right for a short paper?
  \item The policy we give the model names the Commission's list of high-risk countries.
        Is that the right choice, or should the model receive no list?
  \item Is the yardstick convincing: a customer who lives and works in Germany, for whom
        the rules give the passport no weight?
  \item Are 20 customer files, four placebo nationalities and five repetitions enough?
  \item Are four open-weight models between 8B and 24B parameters acceptable, although
        the products in this field run on larger commercial models?
  \item The prompts are in English although the bank is German. Is that a problem?
  \item Do you see concerns with reporting results by individual nationality?
\end{enumerate}
