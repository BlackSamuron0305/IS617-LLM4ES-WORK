\section{Research questions}
\label{sec:rq}

We give an LLM a job and CVs that are identical except for one line stating the
nationality, and observe what it decides. The 22 Arab League countries differ widely in
wealth, in political stability and in how their citizens are seen abroad: high-income
Gulf states, middle-income countries such as Egypt, Jordan and Morocco, and countries
marked by war and displacement such as Syria, Yemen, Sudan, Libya and Somalia. We ask
whether a model carries these differences into decisions about individual people.

\begin{description}[leftmargin=2.6em,labelwidth=2.1em,labelsep=0.5em]
  \item[RQ1] Do fit scores and decisions differ across the 22 Arab League nationalities
        when everything else is identical, and is this spread larger than for unrelated
        placebo nationalities? This is the main question.
  \item[RQ2] Do the differences follow a country's economic and political position, for
        example Gulf nationalities above nationalities associated with war, displacement
        or low income?
  \item[RQ3] Does the place of the job matter? Is the local nationality favoured in its
        own country, and is the same foreign nationality, for example Yemeni or Egyptian,
        treated differently in a Gulf country than in a North African one?
  \item[RQ4] Do the differences remain when the model is told that nationality is not a
        criterion, and when it may let a lottery decide between two identical candidates?
  \item[RQ5] Are the patterns the same in hiring and in promotion, and across models?
\end{description}

\noindent The comparison between Arab and non-Arab nationalities is secondary.

\section{Why the question is realistic and open}
\label{sec:related}

\textbf{The scenario is common.} Many Arabs work in an Arab country other than their own.
In the Gulf states, Arabs from other countries were 72\% of the foreign population in
1975 and still about a third in the early 2000s \citep{kapiszewski2006arab}. In Jordan,
Jordanian, Egyptian and Syrian workers share one labour market
\citep{razzaz2017challenging}. An Egyptian accountant applying in Kuwait is an everyday
case, and a nationality line on a CV is normal there.

\textbf{Nationality is used openly.} A report for the International Labour Organization
finds that segmentation in Jordan is based on nationality rather than on education or
skills \citep{razzaz2017challenging}. Among low-income workers in Qatar, pay differs by
nationality \citep{gardner2013portrait}. Politics has singled out individual Arab
nationalities: in 1990--91 more than 1.5 million people were expelled from Gulf states,
among them up to one million Yemenis from Saudi Arabia, 200,000 Jordanians and 150,000
Palestinians \citep{kapiszewski2006arab}.

\textbf{Models hold country-specific associations.} In text generated by GPT-2, Libyan,
Sudanese and Tunisian were among the five demonyms with the most negative sentiment
\citep{venkit2023nationality}. In simulated immigration decisions, GPT models suggested
additional scrutiny for applicants from Iraq, Sudan and Somalia
\citep{mao2025gatekeepers}. Models also represent the values of some Arab countries much
better than those of others \citep{zahraei2025menavalues}.

\textbf{The answer is open in both directions.} Field experiments in Western labour
markets find about 41\% fewer positive callbacks for applicants with Arab, Maghrebi or
Middle Eastern names \citep{lippens2023state}, and origin can matter within that group:
Swedish employers called back 10\% of Iraq-born and 5\% of Somalia-born applicants
\citep{vernby2019immigrants}. Inside the region the evidence points the other way.
Qatari citizens made no significant distinction between nationalities in a survey
experiment on residency \citep{shockley2024sharing}, and Arab expatriates in Kuwait and
Qatar favour ``fellow Arabs'' as one group \citep{blaydes2023arab}. A result of ``no
difference'' would therefore also be an answer.

\textbf{Existing LLM audits cannot tell.} Audits of LLMs in hiring mostly vary race and
gender through names \citep{gaebler2024auditing,armstrong2024silicon}. Where Arab
applicants appear, they are one group: a penalty of 1.41 points on a 100-point scale for
Arab names in GPT-3.5 \citep{lippens2024computer}, and an average effect close to zero in
a more recent study \citep{hoffmann2026evaluating}. This follows from the method, because
a name does not identify a country: raters assigned only 34\% of Moroccan names to
Morocco \citep{martiniello2022signaling}. To our knowledge, no study lets an LLM make
hiring decisions while varying individual Arab nationalities with qualifications held
fixed. The closest one we found pools them into Arab versus non-Arab
\citep{albaroudi2026addressing}. We treat this claim as tentative.

\section{Design}
\label{sec:design}

\textbf{Materials.} Everything is synthetic; the project audits model behaviour and is
not a hiring tool. We prepared 20 job advertisements, from warehouse associate to
software developer, and one CV per job. Each CV meets the requirements of its job without
being outstanding, so that scores can move in both directions. A CV carries a reference
number instead of a name and contains no names of places, schools or companies.

\textbf{Manipulation.} One line in the CV, for example \texttt{Nationality: Jordanian}.
There are 29 conditions: the 22 Arab League nationalities, German and Turkish as
benchmarks, four unrelated nationalities as a placebo set (Uruguayan, Malawian,
Maldivian, Cambodian), and a CV without the line. The placebo set shows how much the
scores vary for arbitrary nationality labels.

\textbf{Setting.} The model is told that it recruits for the company in a given country.
Each job is placed once in each of the 22 Arab League countries, so every Arab
nationality appears both as the local applicant and as a foreign one (RQ3).

\textbf{Tasks.} (1)~Rate one applicant: a fit score from 0 to 100 and interview yes or
no. (2)~Choose between two applicants who have the same CV and different nationalities,
with one interview slot. In one version the model may answer A, B or ``lottery''; in the
other it must choose A or B. Each pair is shown in both orders. (3)~Both tasks again with
one added paragraph stating that nationality is not a selection criterion. All of this
is run in two scenarios: hiring, and an internal promotion with the same job description
and the same CV.

\textbf{Models.} Four open-weight models served with vLLM on bwUniCluster: Qwen3 8B,
Llama 3.1 8B, Gemma 3 12B and Mistral Small 3.2 24B.

\textbf{Size.} Table~\ref{tab:runs} lists the runs per model. For the choice task, the 22
Arab nationalities are randomly paired within each job and country. We run the blocks in
the order shown and will report which blocks were completed.

\section{What we will compare}
\label{sec:analysis}

\begin{description}[leftmargin=2.6em,labelwidth=2.1em,labelsep=0.5em]
  \item[RQ1] How far the mean score and the decision rates differ across the 22 Arab
        nationalities, against the spread across the placebo nationalities.
  \item[RQ2] Each nationality's result against its country's income group (World Bank)
        and against whether the country is affected by armed conflict. The conflict
        classification still has to be fixed.
  \item[RQ3] Local versus foreign applicants, and each foreign nationality across the
        countries of the job.
  \item[RQ4] The same comparisons with the added paragraph; in the choice task, which
        nationality is chosen, how often the model takes the lottery, and what it does
        when the lottery is removed.
  \item[RQ5] Hiring against promotion, and the four models against each other.
\end{description}

\noindent We have not fixed the statistical analysis yet. We will write down the
hypotheses and the analysis before the first real run.

\section{Questions}
\label{sec:questions}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Is the scope right for a short paper, or should we drop the promotion scenario
        and keep hiring only?
  \item For RQ2, which classification of the countries would you use (income, conflict,
        region), and is it defensible to group them at all?
  \item Is one CV per job at one qualification level enough, or would you expect several
        levels?
  \item Is the choice between two identical CVs with a lottery option a sensible measure,
        and are four placebo nationalities enough as a yardstick?
  \item Are four open-weight models between 8B and 24B parameters acceptable?
  \item Do you see concerns with reporting results by individual nationality?
\end{enumerate}
