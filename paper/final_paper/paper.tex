\begin{abstract}
% Rubric [5]: 250-word summary of your project including a formulation of its
% research questions and an overview of methods and results.
\teamnote{abstract}{%
At most 250 words. The rubric asks for the research questions, the methods and the
results. In all notes, (F) after a source means the full text was read and (A) means
only the abstract was read: check the full text before citing a number from it.
\begin{itemize}
  \item Context: LLMs are proposed for screening job applications. Audits so far mostly
        vary race and gender through names.
  \item Why Arab applicants: human employers in Western countries give about 41\% fewer
        callbacks to Arab, North African and Middle Eastern names. LLMs repeat
        stereotypes about Arabs and Muslims in what they write. In hiring tasks the
        evidence is thin and mixed. Numbers and sources: introduction notes.
  \item Why inside the Arab world: nationality openly sorts workers there, and LLM
        recruiting tools are being built for these markets.
  \item Gap: every study treats ``Arab'' as one group. To our knowledge, none lets an
        LLM decide on applicants while single Arab nationalities are varied.
  \item Questions (from the proposal; hypotheses are not written yet): (1) With the CV
        held fixed, do a model's ratings and choices differ across the 22 Arab League
        nationalities, and by more than across unrelated nationalities? (2) Local
        versus foreign applicants. (3) Does a paragraph telling the model to ignore
        nationality change this? (4) Do hiring and promotion, and the four models, agree?
  \item Method: 20 synthetic jobs with one CV each; every job placed in each of the 22
        Arab League countries; one \texttt{Nationality:} line with 29 conditions; rate
        one applicant (score 0--100, interview yes/no); choose between two applicants
        with the same CV; four open-weight models.
  \item Results: nothing has been run as of 2026-10-04. Write this part last, and only
        from the blocks that were run.
  \item Last sentence: what follows. The data decide: either the single category hides
        differences, or it is adequate for these models.
\end{itemize}}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% Rubric "Motivation" [5]: What question(s) do you seek to answer and why? Elaborate on
% how this research matters in terms of science, technology and/or society.

\teamnote{motivation, step by step}{%
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{1. LLMs in hiring.} Many audits of LLMs that screen CVs; almost all vary
        race and gender through names. Nationality is rare: it appears in 13.2\% of 189
        papers on LLM bias, gender in 79.9\% (not specific to hiring). Sources:
        \texttt{gaebler2024auditing} (F), \texttt{armstrong2024silicon} (F),
        \texttt{an2025measuring} (A), \texttt{ghosh2025bias} (A).
  \item \textbf{2. Human employers.} Meta-analysis of field experiments: callback ratio
        0.59 for Arab, Maghrebi and Middle Eastern applicants, about 41\% fewer positive
        callbacks, from 31 effects. Higher after 2000 than in the 1990s in six Western
        countries. France: 22.8\% vs 33.3\% callbacks (9,600 applications; a report, not
        a journal article). Netherlands: 31\% vs 46\%. Australia: 22\% vs 35\%. Sweden,
        Iraq-born: 10\% vs 21\%. Sources: \texttt{lippens2023state} (F),
        \texttt{quillian2023trends} (F), \texttt{arnoult2021discrimination} (F),
        \texttt{thijssen2021ethnic} (F), \texttt{booth2012does} (F),
        \texttt{vernby2019immigrants} (F).
  \item \textbf{3. LLMs.} In generated text: Muslims tied to violence in 66\% of GPT-3
        completions; Arabs rather than Westerners named the ``loser group'' in 79\% of
        model-by-topic comparisons (the prompt forces a choice); MENA names 78\%
        under-represented in generated workplace stories. In hiring tasks: Arab names
        1.41 points lower on a 1--100 scale in GPT-3.5 (selection ratio 0.85 at a
        cut-off); GPT-4 sends Arabic names to the lower-status job more often; no effect
        in Gemini 2.0 Flash; some newer models favour the minority. Sources:
        \texttt{abid2021persistent} (A), \texttt{saeed2024desert} (A),
        \texttt{shieh2026intersectional} (F), \texttt{lippens2024computer} (F),
        \texttt{bai2025explicitly} (A), \texttt{hoffmann2026evaluating} (F),
        \texttt{bunel2026customer} (F), \texttt{arcuschin2026blind} (F).
  \item \textbf{4. One category.} All of these treat ``Arab'' as one group. A name cannot
        do better: raters assigned 34\% of Moroccan names to Morocco. Among human
        employers, origin can matter (Germany: Iraqi and Moroccan below Turkish; Sweden:
        Iraq-born 10\%, Somalia-born 5\%). Inside the Arab world the little evidence
        points the other way: no significant nationality differences among Qatari
        citizens; Arab expatriates in Kuwait and Qatar treat fellow Arabs as one group.
        Sources: \texttt{martiniello2022signaling} (F), \texttt{koopmans2019taste} (F),
        \texttt{vernby2019immigrants} (F), \texttt{shockley2024sharing} (F),
        \texttt{blaydes2023arab} (F).
  \item \textbf{5. Why the Arab world.} Nationality openly sorts workers there. Qatar,
        low-income workers: Egyptian QR1,454 vs Nepali QR853 a month (plain averages).
        Jordan: segmentation ``based not on education or initial skills but rather on
        nationality''. Gulf states expelled workers by Arab nationality in 1990--91. An
        LLM recruiting pipeline exists for the Saudi market; it codes nationality as
        Arab vs non-Arab. Sources: \texttt{gardner2013portrait} (F),
        \texttt{razzaz2017challenging} (F, ILO report), \texttt{kapiszewski2006arab}
        (F, UN paper), \texttt{albaroudi2026addressing} (F).
  \item \textbf{6. Gap and what we do.} No study was found in which an LLM decides on
        applicants while single Arab nationalities are varied (searches of 29--30.09 and
        04.10.2026; \texttt{research/novelty\_assessment.md}). Write ``to our
        knowledge''. Then the question and one sentence on the design.
\end{itemize}}

\teamnote{research questions (as in the proposal of 2026-10-04)}{%
\begin{itemize}
  \item Framing: the 22 countries differ in wealth, stability and how their citizens are
        seen abroad (high-income Gulf states; middle-income Egypt, Jordan, Morocco;
        countries marked by war and displacement such as Syria, Yemen, Sudan, Libya,
        Somalia). Does a model carry that into decisions about individuals?
  \item RQ1 (main): do scores and decisions differ across the 22 Arab League
        nationalities with everything else identical, and more than across the placebo
        nationalities?
  \item RQ2: do the differences follow a country's economic and political position
        (income group, armed conflict)? The conflict classification is not fixed yet.
  \item RQ3: does the place of the job matter: local versus foreign, and the same
        foreign nationality in a Gulf country versus a North African one?
  \item RQ4: do the differences remain with the ignore-nationality paragraph, and when a
        lottery is allowed?
  \item RQ5: same patterns in hiring and promotion, and across models?
\end{itemize}}

\teamnote{why the scenario is realistic}{%
\begin{itemize}
  \item Many Arabs work in another Arab country. Gulf states: Arabs from other countries
        were 72\% of the foreign population in 1975, 56\% in 1985 and 32\% in 2002/4.
        Jordan: Jordanian, Egyptian and Syrian workers in one labour market. Sources:
        \texttt{kapiszewski2006arab} (F, UN paper), \texttt{razzaz2017challenging}
        (F, ILO report).
  \item Politics has singled out single Arab nationalities: 1990--91, more than 1.5
        million people expelled from Gulf states, among them up to one million Yemenis
        from Saudi Arabia, 200,000 Jordanians and 150,000 Palestinians. Source:
        \texttt{kapiszewski2006arab} (F).
  \item Models hold country-specific associations: Libyan, Sudanese and Tunisian among
        the five most negative demonyms in GPT-2 text; GPT models suggest additional
        scrutiny for applicants from Iraq, Sudan and Somalia in simulated immigration
        decisions; models represent the values of some Arab countries much better than
        others. Sources: \texttt{venkit2023nationality} (F),
        \texttt{mao2025gatekeepers} (F), \texttt{zahraei2025menavalues} (F).
  \item No source yet for Arabs working in Egypt or in North African countries. Find one
        before writing that.
\end{itemize}}

\teamnote{why it matters (rubric: science, technology, society)}{%
\begin{itemize}
  \item Science: bias audits report one number for ``Arab''. If single nationalities
        differ, that number hides it. If they do not, pooling is justified for these
        models. Either result answers the question.
  \item Technology: LLM screening tools are proposed for Arab labour markets
        (\texttt{albaroudi2026addressing}, F). How they react to a nationality line is
        untested.
  \item Society: nationality already sorts workers in these markets (step 5). A screener
        that ranks nationalities would repeat that at scale. This last sentence is our
        own argument and has no source.
\end{itemize}}

\teamnote{do not overstate}{%
\begin{itemize}
  \item Not ``LLMs discriminate against Arab applicants'' as a fact: one small penalty,
        one null, some reversals.
  \item The callback numbers are from Western countries. No field experiment with
        applicant nationality was found inside an Arab country.
  \item Not ``Arab nationalities are treated differently in Arab countries'': that is
        barely studied. Say that nationality matters in these labour markets.
  \item Say ``Arab League nationalities'', not ``MENA'' and not ``Arab applicants''. MENA
        in other studies includes Turkey or Iran.
  \item The claim to be first is tentative. One paper is still unread in full
        (\texttt{bilon2025sociodemographic}).
  \item Do not cite numbers from \texttt{mohammad2026mirage}: they are placeholders.
  \item This is an audit of model behaviour, not a hiring tool. The score is not a
        hiring probability.
\end{itemize}}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
% Rubric "Research background" [5]: What previous work is related to this project and
% what can we learn from it? Do not just cite papers but highlight what's relevant to
% your work. Reflect about the cited contributions on your text.

\teamnote{what each strand teaches us}{%
The rubric asks for what is relevant to our work, not for a list of papers.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{LLM hiring audits.} Mostly US race and gender through names
        (\texttt{gaebler2024auditing}, F; \texttt{armstrong2024silicon}, F). The
        direction is unstable: in one study only the 2023 model favours White applicants
        and every later model shows no gap or the reverse (\texttt{gao2026can}, A); in
        another, every detected race or ethnicity bias favours the minority
        (\texttt{arcuschin2026blind}, F). For us: expect either direction.
  \item \textbf{Order and ties.} The first-listed candidate is chosen 63.5\% of the time
        (\texttt{rozado2026gender}, F): we show each pair in both orders. With identical
        CVs, models mostly call a tie when they may (at least 94\% ties for 7 of 9
        models), and forbidding ties creates apparent gaps (\texttt{chen2026beyond}, F;
        \texttt{vohra2026audit}, F). This is why experiment 2 has a lottery version and
        a must-choose version, and why the must-choose version needs care.
  \item \textbf{Explicit cues.} A stated attribute moves decisions more than a name
        (\texttt{tamkin2023evaluating}, F). Different cues for the same group can give
        different conclusions (\texttt{tonneau2026cues}, A). For us: the results hold
        for a nationality line, not for names.
  \item \textbf{Arab group in LLM studies.} Always pooled:
        \texttt{lippens2024computer} (F), \texttt{hoffmann2026evaluating} (F),
        \texttt{bai2025explicitly} (A), \texttt{albaroudi2026addressing} (F).
        Stereotypes in text: \texttt{abid2021persistent} (A), \texttt{saeed2024desert}
        (A), \texttt{shieh2026intersectional} (F).
  \item \textbf{Nationality in LLM decisions.} 20 nationalities in job recommendations,
        Jordan the only Arab one; the condition without a nationality behaved like an
        outlier (\texttt{salinas2023unequal}, F). For us: the CV without a nationality
        line is not a neutral zero. GPT-4 prefers developers from some countries, tested
        by swapping only the location string (\texttt{nakano2024nigerian}, F).
  \item \textbf{Telling the model to ignore the attribute.} Brought discrimination close
        to zero in Claude 2.0 (\texttt{tamkin2023evaluating}, F). The best instruction
        cut the gap between groups by 27\% on average
        (\texttt{huijzer2025discrimination}, F). Instructions fail once realistic
        company context is added (\texttt{karvonen2025robustly}, F). For us: experiment
        3 can go either way.
  \item \textbf{Human evidence.} See the introduction notes, steps 2, 4 and 5.
  \item \textbf{Promotion.} No source on promotion decisions has been searched yet. Find
        one, or justify the scenario in a sentence.
\end{itemize}}

\section{Data}
\label{sec:data}
% Rubric [10]: Describe your data sources including descriptive statistics and plots.
% Carefully document all references to data sources.

\teamnote{materials}{%
\begin{itemize}
  \item 20 job ads, one per occupation: 7 low-skill, 6 mid, 2 mid-high, 5 high.
        Employer names are invented. Every ad requires English only.
  \item 20 CVs, one per job. Middle qualification level: meets the requirements, not
        outstanding. No names of places, schools or companies.
  \item Setting: every job is set once in each of the 22 Arab League countries.
  \item Nationality: one line in the CV. 29 conditions: 22 Arab League nationalities,
        2 benchmarks (German, Turkish), 4 placebo nationalities (Uruguayan, Malawian,
        Maldivian, Cambodian), 1 without a nationality line.
  \item Everything is synthetic: no real applicant, CV or employer.
  \item Still needed for the rubric: descriptive statistics and plots of the answers.
\end{itemize}
Source: the README files under \texttt{experiment/}.}

\teamnote{why the CVs carry no names}{%
The CV shows a reference number instead of a name. Example of a CV header:
\begin{quote}\ttfamily
Applicant reference: APP-4106\\
Location: Jordan\\
Nationality: Egyptian
\end{quote}
\begin{itemize}
  \item A name cannot tell Arab countries apart. It signals ``Arab'' as one group only;
        the nationality line can signal each of the 22.
  \item A name adds signals we do not want: gender, religion, sometimes region.
  \item The same name would be on every version of a CV, also next to a non-Arab
        nationality, which sends a mixed signal.
  \item A missing name is not neutral either, so the main prompt tells the model that
        applications are pseudonymised.
\end{itemize}
Sources in \texttt{custom.bib}: \texttt{armstrong2024silicon}, \texttt{chen2026beyond},
\texttt{gaebler2024auditing}, \texttt{krause2012anonymous} (only the abstract was read).}

\teamnote{experiment setup}{%
\begin{itemize}
  \item One run: a main prompt (the model is an HR recruiter for the company in the
        country), then a follow-up message with the job ad and the CV(s).
  \item Experiment 1, rate one person: fit score 0--100 and yes/no.
  \item Experiment 2, choose between two people with the same CV and different
        nationalities. Two versions: A, B or lottery; A or B.
  \item Experiment 3: experiments 1 and 2 again, with one added paragraph in the main
        prompt telling the model to ignore nationality.
  \item Two scenarios: hiring (interview) and promotion.
  \item Three wordings of every follow-up message, used in rotation.
  \item Pairs for experiment 2: per job and country, the 22 Arab nationalities are
        randomly paired into 11 pairs; each pair is shown in both orders.
  \item Models: Qwen3 8B, Llama 3.1 8B, Gemma 3 12B, Mistral Small 3.2 24B. State the
        exact versions and settings.
  \item How the answers are analysed: not decided as of 2026-10-04.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion and Discussion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% Rubric [5]: Evaluate answers to the question and their reliability. Identify
% limitations and alternative explanations for your results.

\teamnote{alternative explanations to discuss}{%
\begin{itemize}
  \item A nationality line also says ``local'' or ``foreigner''. Every job is run in
        every country, so the two can be separated: compare the local nationality with
        the others. In human hiring, lacking citizenship costs callbacks
        (\texttt{quillian2026racialized}, A).
  \item The label may stand for religion, conflict or the income of the country, not
        for the nationality as such. \texttt{country\_covariates.csv} has income and
        region.
  \item Any set of labels spreads the scores a little. The placebo nationalities are the
        yardstick; with only four of them it is a rough one.
  \item Models may notice that this is a test (\texttt{vohra2026audit}, F;
        \texttt{needham2025large}, A).
  \item One run per combination and no repeats: the chance variation of a single answer
        is not measured on its own.
  \item A result of ``no difference'' is an answer too. It would fit the little human
        evidence from inside the Arab world (\texttt{shockley2024sharing}, F;
        \texttt{blaydes2023arab}, F).
\end{itemize}}
