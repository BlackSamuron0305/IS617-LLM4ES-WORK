% research questions and an overview of methods and results.
\teamnote{abstract}{%
At most 250 words. The rubric asks for the research questions, the methods and the
results. In all notes, (F) after a source means the text or data file itself was read,
at least in the passages cited; (A) means only the abstract was read, or the page was
read through a summarising tool: check the original before citing a number or a quote
from it. (press) is a press report, (party) a statement by one side of a dispute.
\begin{itemize}
  \item Motivation: Islamophobia. Muslims in Europe report more discrimination than ten
        years ago, and language models repeat stereotypes about Muslims. Numbers and
        sources: introduction notes.
  \item Why passports: a visa form asks for no religion. It asks for a passport. All
        Muslim-majority countries together are too mixed (53 countries). The 22 Arab
        League countries share a language and a label.
  \item The question: bias among Arabs. Same application, one applicant Syrian, one
        Emirati. The official rules for the visa are the same for both. Why should a
        model rate them differently?
  \item Why visas: automated tools already sort and rate visa applicants, nationality
        has been an input, Australia itself uses machine learning to ``predict risk in
        visa programs'', and the EU AI Act treats such systems as high-risk. No
        authority is documented as using a language model for it.
  \item Gap: we found no study that changes only the nationality in a visa application,
        and none that compares the 22 nationalities; this rests on limited searches.
  \item Questions (from the proposal of 2026-10-10; hypotheses are not written yet):
        (1) do a model's ratings differ across the 22 Arab League nationalities when the
        application is identical and the official rules are the same for all of them?
        (2) Can the 22 countries be grouped into rich, normal and poor by how the models
        rate them? (3) Does a paragraph saying that nationality is not a ground for a
        higher rating change this? (4) What do the differences cost an applicant?
  \item Method: 20 synthetic applications for an Australian visitor visa, from people
        born and living in the United Arab Emirates; 26 nationalities (the 22 and four
        unrelated ones as a noise check), changed in the nationality and passport lines
        only; a one-page guidance in two versions; risk score 0--100, rating, further
        checks, recommendation; four open-weight models.
  \item Results: nothing has been run as of 2026-10-10. Write this part last, and only
        from the blocks that were run.
  \item Last sentence: what follows. The data decide: the models treat the 22 alike, as
        the rules do, or they do not.
\end{itemize}}

\teamnote{motivation, step by step}{%
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{1. Islamophobia in numbers.} 13 EU countries, survey of 2021--22: 47\%
        of Muslim respondents report racial discrimination in the past five years
        (2016: 39\%); Germany 68\%. The survey's term covers skin colour, origin and
        religion together. Germany: 3,080 anti-Muslim incidents documented in 2024,
        1,926 in 2023; the number of reporting centres rose from 17 to 26, so part of
        the rise is more counting. The police counted 1,848 offences in 2024. Sources:
        \texttt{fra2024being} (A), \texttt{claim2025lagebild} (F, short summary),
        \texttt{bundestag2026islamfeindlichkeit} (F, first pages; the figures are in the
        question, not in the government's answer).
  \item \textbf{2. LLMs and Muslims.} 66 of 100 GPT-3 completions of ``Two Muslims
        walked into a'' were violent; later GPT models show a weaker form, stronger for
        Muslim names than for the word ``Muslim''. Models also tell Arab countries
        apart: Libya, Sudan and Tunisia among the five most negative demonyms in GPT-2
        text. Sources: \texttt{abid2021persistent} (F), \texttt{hemmatian2023muslim}
        (F), \texttt{venkit2023nationality} (F).
  \item \textbf{3. From a religion to a passport.} The Australian visa form asks for the
        country of the passport, the place of birth, other citizenships, the country the
        person lives in and the legal status there. It asks for no religion. Link to the
        motivation: in a hiring experiment in five European countries, applicants from
        Muslim-majority countries were at a disadvantage in four of them even without
        any sign of religion. Against the link: in a US survey experiment people
        penalised the label ``Muslim'' but not the label ``Arab''; in a study of asylum
        preferences religion counted for more than the country. Sources:
        \texttt{homeaffairs2018form1419} (F, old paper form in a third-party copy),
        \texttt{distasio2021muslim} (F), \texttt{calfano2021evaluating} (A),
        \texttt{bansak2016economic} (F).
  \item \textbf{4. Why the 22.} 53 countries had a Muslim majority in 2020, on three
        continents. The 22 Arab League members are all among them (lowest share:
        Lebanon, 68\%), share a language and are commonly put under one label. Source:
        \texttt{pew2025religious} (F, data file).
  \item \textbf{5. The yardstick: the official rules are the same for all 22.} We do not
        ask how strong a passport is. For the Australian visitor visa: none of the 22
        can use the two electronic permits, so all need the same visa; the same online
        form; the same fee (AUD 250); the same legal test (genuine intention to stay
        temporarily, adequate means). The law allows a list of risk groups by
        nationality; the last list was revoked in 2009 and none was found in force. No
        ban or suspension for any of the 22 as of 10 October 2026. The department's
        instruction (version of 2016): a profile is ``merely an alert'', ``is not
        grounds to refuse to grant a visa'', and ``all applications must be considered
        on their own merits''. So identical applications should get the same assessment;
        a systematic difference among the 22 is what we call bias. Sources:
        \texttt{homeaffairs2026eta} (F), \texttt{homeaffairs2026evisitor} (F),
        \texttt{au2021lin21056} (F), \texttt{au1994migrationregs} (F, named clauses),
        \texttt{au2008riskfactor} (F), \texttt{au2009revocation} (F),
        \texttt{homeaffairs2018genguide} (F, the sections on genuine temporary stay and
        criterion 4011). Table: \texttt{experiment/countries/official\_rules.csv}.
  \item \textbf{6. Why Australia.} The only destination checked where the rules are the
        same for all 22. Elsewhere the rules sort the nationalities: Emirati citizens
        need no visa for the Schengen area; Gulf citizens travel to the United Kingdom,
        Canada and New Zealand on an electronic permit; the United States bars six of
        the 22 by proclamation. There a different answer follows from a rule and is not
        a bias. Sources: \texttt{crs2025proclamation} (A), research note
        \texttt{destination\_and\_requirements.md}.
  \item \textbf{7. Same rules, different outcomes in practice.} Australian visitor visas
        granted in 2019--20: 96.5\% for Omani, 21.9\% for Somali citizens (our
        calculation from the counts). Granted applications decided at the median in
        about one day for the Gulf states, in 29 days for Syrian and 30 days for
        Palestinian applicants. US visitor visas refused in fiscal year 2025: 2.2\% of
        Emirati, 83.5\% of Somali applicants. These are real applicants in different
        circumstances: they show what a model may have learned, not a bias by
        themselves. Sources: \texttt{homeaffairs2020foi106} (F),
        \texttt{homeaffairs2025da449} (F), \texttt{state2025refusal} (F).
  \item \textbf{8. Machines already sort and rate visa applicants} (extended on
        2026-10-12; the exact wording of every source is in the note after this one).
        \emph{In use.} United Kingdom 2015--2020: green, amber, red; 96.36\% of green and
        48.59\% of red visit applications issued in one office in early 2017;
        nationality was one factor; stopped in August 2020 after a legal challenge.
        Netherlands: profiles with nationality, age group and gender; 33\% rejected on
        the intensive track, 3.5\% on the normal one. Canada: sorts all visitor visa
        applications from abroad into tiers, approves routine ones, ``never refuses
        applications nor does it recommend refusals''. \textbf{Australia, our
        destination}: analytics based on machine learning are used to ``predict risk in
        visa programs''; the department says it ``does not use machine learning to make
        administrative decisions''; what goes into the prediction is not public. United
        States: a pilot gives visa cases that are already decided a score from 0 to 1
        for review by managers; it is ``not used as an applicant risk determination''.
        \emph{Planned.} EU travel authorisation for visitors without a visa: ``an
        algorithm enabling profiling'' on ``age range, sex, nationality''; start
        scheduled for the last quarter of 2026. Germany: a bill on AI in migration
        offices, first reading on 8 October 2026. South Africa: one press report of a
        plan to let machine learning take yes or no decisions on travel permits.
        \emph{What kind of software.} The British tool was a decision tree and the Dutch
        one is rules written by officials. Machine learning is named in the Australian
        document and for the US pilot; Canada speaks of ``advanced analytics''. EU law
        classes systems that assist in examining visa applications as high-risk. None of
        these is a language model. Sources: \texttt{icibi2017entry} (F),
        \texttt{ukhrb2020streaming} (F, secondary), \texttt{foxglove2020visa} (party),
        \texttt{lighthouse2023ethnic} (F in the passages used, journalism),
        \texttt{nlminister2023iob} (F, two answers), \texttt{ircc2022aia} (F),
        \texttt{ircc2025qpnote} (F), \texttt{homeaffairs2024foi} (F),
        \texttt{state2026aiinventory} (F), \texttt{eu2018etias} (F, one article),
        \texttt{meier2026algorithmic} (F), \texttt{aabmi2025kieckpunkte} (F),
        \texttt{bundestag2026kimvg} (F, one paragraph), \texttt{mawson2026machine}
        (press), \texttt{eu2021vis} (A), \texttt{eu2024aiact} (A).
  \item \textbf{9. Why the applicants live in the United Arab Emirates} (Laith's
        decision of 2026-10-10: the most realistic setting). Birth there gives no
        citizenship to the child of two foreign parents. 88.5\% of the residents were
        foreigners in the last official figures that give the split. 298,064
        applications for a Schengen visa were made in the Emirates in 2025, although
        Emirati citizens need none; 24.2\% were not issued (our calculation). Every
        application states the same legal status: a lawful long-term resident with the
        full right to live and work there. Such a status exists for foreigners, for
        example the Golden Visa: ``a long-term, renewable residence visa valid for 5 or
        10 years'' without a sponsor; it is not given by birth. Sources:
        \texttt{uae1972nationality} (A, English abstract of the law),
        \texttt{khaleej2011expats} (press, reporting the statistics office),
        \texttt{ec2026visa} (F, data file), \texttt{uae2026goldenvisa} (A).
  \item \textbf{10. Gap and what we do.} Closest study: GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 choose between
        two immigrants; ten countries, among them Iraq, Sudan and Somalia; most country
        effects not significant. A public test set has a visitor visa case but varies
        age, gender and race. No study was found that changes only the nationality in a
        visa application or compares the 22. This rests on limited searches of 10
        October 2026: write ``to our knowledge'' only after a systematic search. Sources:
        \texttt{mao2025gatekeepers} (F), \texttt{tamkin2023evaluating} (F),
        \texttt{anthropic2023discrimeval} (F, data files).

\teamnote{why it matters (rubric: science, technology, society)}{%
\begin{itemize}
  \item Science: bias audits report one number for ``Arab'' or ``Muslim''. Here the
        question is whether a model treats 22 Arab nationalities alike when the rules
        say it should. Nationality is rare in this literature: 13.2\% of 189 papers on
        LLM bias, religion 19.1\%, gender 79.9\% (\texttt{ghosh2025bias}, A).
  \item Technology: sorting tools for visa applications exist and have used nationality
        (step 8). How language models react to the passport line is untested, as far as
        we found.
  \item Society: a refusal costs the fee (AUD 250, not refunded), has to be declared in
        later applications, and stops visits to family, study and work. Fees paid for
        refused Schengen visas: EUR 157.1 million in 2025 according to one collective.
        Sources: \texttt{au1994migrationregs} (F), \texttt{homeaffairs2026refunds} (F),
        \texttt{foresti2026cost} (A, advocacy).
\end{itemize}}

\teamnote{what each strand teaches us}{%
The rubric asks for what is relevant to our work, not for a list of papers.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The closest study.} A model as US immigration officer chooses between
        two applicants; nine attributes vary at once; one prompt; two OpenAI models.
        Country mattered little in the choices, but asked directly the models named
        Iraq, Sudan and Somalia for ``additional scrutiny''
        (\texttt{mao2025gatekeepers}, F). For us: rate one applicant instead of choosing
        between two, change only the nationality, use several wordings and open models,
        and expect small effects in the choice and more in the stated reasons.
  \item \textbf{LLM audits with one explicit line.} Mortgage files with a race line:
        gaps in approval and interest rate. Llama 3 8B approved every application, so
        approval could not be analysed for it. Claude 3 Opus answered only 74\% of the
        requests for Black applicants (\texttt{bowen2025measuring}, F). For us: a yes/no
        answer may not move in small models, hence the 0--100 score; count refusals to
        answer by nationality.
  \item \textbf{A gap expressed in something valuable.} The same paper: Black applicants
        would need a credit score about 52 points higher for the same approval rate
        (introduction; the results section of the same version says 120 points: quote
        the page or ask the authors). In human studies: 71 credit-score points, eight
        years of experience (\texttt{hanson2016discrimination}, A;
        \texttt{francis2003employers}, A). For us: the model for RQ4.
  \item \textbf{Direction is unstable.} Real origin labels led to fewer discriminatory
        recommendations than invented groups (\texttt{bunel2026customer}, F); in one
        study an explicit Muslim label did slightly better than other religions in loan
        approval (\texttt{arcuschin2026blind}, F). For us: expect either direction.
  \item \textbf{Explicit cues.} A stated attribute moves decisions more than a name
        (\texttt{tamkin2023evaluating}, F), but for Muslims a name triggered more than
        the word (\texttt{hemmatian2023muslim}, F). Different cues for the same group
        can give different conclusions (\texttt{tonneau2026cues}, A). For us: the
        results hold for a nationality and passport line, not for names.
  \item \textbf{Telling the model to ignore the attribute.} Brought discrimination close
        to zero in Claude 2.0 (\texttt{tamkin2023evaluating}, F) and removed the
        approval gap in mortgage files (\texttt{bowen2025measuring}, F). Instructions
        fail once realistic context is added in hiring (\texttt{karvonen2025robustly},
        F). For us: the second version of the guidance can go either way.
  \item \textbf{Poorer countries are described worse.} GPT-2 text is more negative for
        countries with fewer internet users and lower economic status
        (\texttt{venkit2023nationality}, F). For us: one reason to expect that the 22
        are not treated alike.
  \item \textbf{Real visa decisions.} Refusal rates for African countries more than
        double those for Europe, not explained by social and economic factors
        (\texttt{thomas2025global}, A, seminar abstract). Student visas: 8 percentage
        points more refusals for predominantly Muslim countries after controls
        (\texttt{maggio2026racial}, A, unpublished conference paper). For us: evidence
        that passports matter beyond the applicant in real decisions.
  \item \textbf{What a reviewer expects.} The format of an audit moves results as much
        as the group does (\texttt{vohra2026audit}, F); wording variants and an
        anti-discrimination instruction as standard (\texttt{gaebler2024auditing}, F);
        uncertainty by item, not by run (\texttt{miller2024adding}, F); models may
        notice a test (\texttt{needham2025large}, A).
  \item \textbf{Not searched systematically.} Research on consular officers'
        discretion, and German-language work on visa refusals. A few sources are in
        \texttt{research/visa\_scenario/}; find more, or say that this literature was
        not covered.
\end{itemize}}

\teamnote{experiment setup}{%

\section{Conclusion and Discussion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% Rubric [5]: Evaluate answers to the question and their reliability. Identify
% limitations and alternative explanations for your results.

\teamnote{alternative explanations to discuss}{%
\begin{itemize}
  \item Where a difference may come from: real offices treat these nationalities
        differently, and the model has read about them. That explains a bias; it does
        not justify a different answer for identical applications under identical rules.
  \item The label may stand for the wealth, conflict or migration history of a country.
        These are reasons for the bias, not excuses for it, and the design does not
        separate them.
  \item ``Rich'' cannot be told apart from ``Gulf'': the rich group is exactly the six
        Gulf states, the same six for which the status line simplifies reality. The
        comparison of normal and poor is free of this.
  \item ``Poor'' cannot be told apart from ``at war'': Somalia, Sudan, Syria and Yemen
        are all at war or just out of one.
  \item Three groups of 6, 12 and 4 countries are small. A grouping found in the ratings
        may fit three groups by chance; say how many of the 22 land in their group and
        what a random sorting would give.
  \item The guidance names ``conditions in the home country'' and defines home country
        as the country where the applicant lives. A model may apply the sentence to the
        passport country because of the wording. A version without the sentence would
        separate this; it is not planned.
  \item The model may know more than the application says: that an Emirati is a citizen
        in the Emirates, that Gulf citizens need no visa there, that many foreign
        residents hold a visa tied to the job. A better rating for the six Gulf
        nationalities may come from that knowledge. The check without them shows how
        much of a result rests on them.
  \item A passport is not a religion. A difference among Arab League passports does not
        prove a reaction to Islam.
  \item Any set of labels spreads the scores a little. The four unrelated nationalities
        are the yardstick for that; with only four it is a rough one.
  \item Models may notice that this is a test (\texttt{vohra2026audit}, F;
        \texttt{needham2025large}, A).
  \item All 20 applicants are ordinary. If a model answers ``low'' and ``grant'' for
        everybody, the rating cannot show a difference; the score may.
  \item A result of ``no difference'' is an answer too: the models then do what the
        rules ask.
\end{itemize}}
