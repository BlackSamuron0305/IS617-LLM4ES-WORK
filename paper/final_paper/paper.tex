\begin{abstract}
% Rubric [5]: 250-word summary of your project including a formulation of its
% research questions and an overview of methods and results.
\teamnote{abstract}{%
At most 250 words. The rubric asks for the research questions, the methods and the
results. In all notes, (F) after a source means the text or data file itself was read,
at least in the passages cited; (A) means only the abstract was read, or the page was
read through a summarising tool: check the original before citing a number or a quote
from it. (press) is a press report, (party) a statement by one side of a dispute.
\begin{itemize}
  \item Motivation: Islamophobia. Muslims in Europe report more discrimination than ten
        years ago, and language models repeat stereotypes about Muslims. Numbers and
        sources: introduction notes.
  \item Why passports: a visa form asks for no religion. It asks for a passport. All
        Muslim-majority countries together are too mixed (53 countries). The 22 Arab
        League countries share a language and a label.
  \item The question: bias among Arabs. Same application, one applicant Syrian, one
        Emirati. The official rules for the visa are the same for both. Why should a
        model rate them differently?
  \item Why visas: automated tools already sort and rate visa applicants, nationality
        has been an input, Australia itself uses machine learning to ``predict risk in
        visa programs'', and the EU AI Act treats such systems as high-risk. No
        authority is documented as using a language model for it.
  \item Gap: we found no study that changes only the nationality in a visa application,
        and none that compares the 22 nationalities; this rests on limited searches.
  \item Questions (from the proposal of 2026-10-10; hypotheses are not written yet):
        (1) do a model's ratings differ across the 22 Arab League nationalities when the
        application is identical and the official rules are the same for all of them?
        (2) Can the 22 countries be grouped into rich, normal and poor by how the models
        rate them? (3) Does a paragraph saying that nationality is not a ground for a
        higher rating change this? (4) What do the differences cost an applicant?
  \item Method: 30 synthetic applications (plus a boundary set of 10) for a Schengen visa to Estonia, lodged by people born and living in the United Arab
        Emirates; 26 nationalities (the 22 and four unrelated ones as a noise check), changed in the nationality and passport lines only; a guidance
        paraphrasing Article 32(1) of the Visa Code, a score from 0 to 100 and a reason, three audited open-weight models (Qwen3 32B, Gemma 4 31B, Mistral Small 3.2 24B)
        and nine more as an exploratory extension; a judge model codes the reasons.
  \item Results (2026-11-03; details in the results note below): among the 21 nationalities that need a visa the highest and the lowest score differ by 1.6 to 3.4
        points in the audited models (permutation test, $p = 0.0002$), against 5 points we called worth noting; Syria, Yemen, Sudan, Iraq, Libya and Somalia are lowest;
        the paragraph ``nationality is not a ground'' does not shrink the gaps; one small extra model (Qwen2.5 7B) shows 29 points and reasons that raise unsupported
        doubts about money for some passports; this is one checkpoint, not a matter of size (the same family at 32B and 72B shows 1.7 points; exploratory runs of 14B and the smaller sizes show 1 to 4).
  \item Last sentence: what follows. The data decide: the models treat the 22 alike, as
        the rules do, or they do not.
\end{itemize}}
\end{abstract}

\teamnote{motivation, step by step}{%
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{1. Islamophobia in numbers.} 13 EU countries, survey of 2021--22: 47\%
        of Muslim respondents report racial discrimination in the past five years
        (2016: 39\%); Germany 68\%. The survey's term covers skin colour, origin and
        religion together. Germany: 3,080 anti-Muslim incidents documented in 2024,
        1,926 in 2023; the number of reporting centres rose from 17 to 26, so part of
        the rise is more counting. The police counted 1,848 offences in 2024. Sources:
        \texttt{fra2024being} (A), \texttt{claim2025lagebild} (F, short summary),
        \texttt{bundestag2026islamfeindlichkeit} (F, first pages; the figures are in the
        question, not in the government's answer).
  \item \textbf{2. LLMs and Muslims.} 66 of 100 GPT-3 completions of ``Two Muslims
        walked into a'' were violent; later GPT models show a weaker form, stronger for
        Muslim names than for the word ``Muslim''. Models also tell Arab countries
        apart: Libya, Sudan and Tunisia among the five most negative demonyms in GPT-2
        text. Sources: \texttt{abid2021persistent} (F), \texttt{hemmatian2023muslim}
        (F), \texttt{venkit2023nationality} (F).
  \item \textbf{3. From a religion to a passport.} The Schengen visa application asks for the travel document, not for a religion; the Australian form 1419 (older design,
        \texttt{homeaffairs2018form1419}, F) asks for the country of the passport, the place of birth, other citizenships, the country the person lives in and the legal
        status there, also no religion. Link to the motivation: in a hiring experiment in five European countries, applicants from Muslim-majority countries were at a
        disadvantage in four of them even without any sign of religion. Against the link: in a US survey experiment people penalised the label ``Muslim'' but not the label
        ``Arab''; in a study of asylum preferences religion counted for more than the country. Sources: \texttt{distasio2021muslim} (F), \texttt{calfano2021evaluating} (A),
        \texttt{bansak2016economic} (F).
  \item \textbf{4. Why the 22.} 53 countries had a Muslim majority in 2020, on three
        continents. The 22 Arab League members are all among them (lowest share:
        Lebanon, 68\%), share a language and are commonly put under one label. Source:
        \texttt{pew2025religious} (F, data file).
  \item \textbf{5. The yardstick: Article 32(1) of the Visa Code.} We do not ask how strong a passport is. Article 32(1) of Regulation (EC) No 810/2009 lists the grounds on which a
        visa shall be refused (false travel document; no justification of the purpose and conditions of the stay; no proof of sufficient means of subsistence; three months
        already stayed in the current six; an alert in the Schengen Information System; a threat to public policy, internal security, public health or international relations;
        no adequate travel medical insurance; reasonable doubts about the documents, the statements or the intention to leave). Nationality is not among them, and a refusal has
        to state its reasons (Article 32(2)). The Estonian ministry's page (read through a fetch tool) gives the fee (EUR 90, not refunded), travel medical insurance of at least
        EUR 30,000 and EUR 70 per day of stay as sufficient means. For the 21 nationalities that need the visa the grounds are the same, so identical applications should get
        the same assessment; a systematic difference is what we call bias. Source text: screenshot supplied by Laith (EUR-Lex layout); \texttt{experiment/inputs/policy/README.md},
        \texttt{experiment/inputs/countries/README.md}. Not read in full text: Articles 14, 15, 21 (risk of illegal immigration), 22 and 25a.
  \item \textbf{6. Why Estonia, and what it costs.} Laith's choice of 2026-11-01: a written list of grounds and the duty to give reasons let a model's reason be held against the
        regulation, and Laith reports that Estonia is adding AI to public services including visa decision support. \textbf{That claim is not confirmed}: searches found the
        national AI programme Eesti.ai and nothing about visas. Cost of the choice: Emirati citizens (and Uruguayans, a placebo) need no Schengen visa, so the rules are the
        same for 21 of the 22 and the two exempt rows are reported apart. The Australian design had the opposite property (same rules for all 22).
  \item \textbf{7. Same rules, different outcomes in practice.} Real consulates refuse and grant nationalities at different rates although the grounds are the same.
        We did not read the Schengen statistics by nationality for this design. For the Australian design (older notes, git history) granted visitor visas in 2019--20 were
        96.5\% for Omani and 21.9\% for Somali citizens (our calculation), and US visitor visas refused in fiscal year 2025 were 2.2\% of Emirati and 83.5\% of Somali
        applicants (\texttt{homeaffairs2020foi106} F, \texttt{state2025refusal} F). These are real applicants in different circumstances: they show what a model may have learned,
        not a bias by themselves.
\end{itemize}}

\teamnote{why it matters (rubric: science, technology, society)}{%
\begin{itemize}
  \item Science: bias audits report one number for ``Arab'' or ``Muslim''. Here the
        question is whether a model treats 22 Arab nationalities alike when the rules
        say it should. Nationality is rare in this literature: 13.2\% of 189 papers on
        LLM bias, religion 19.1\%, gender 79.9\% (\texttt{ghosh2025bias}, A).
  \item Technology: sorting tools for visa applications exist and have used nationality
        (step 8). How language models react to the passport line is untested, as far as
        we found.
  \item Society: a refusal costs the fee (AUD 250, not refunded), has to be declared in
        later applications, and stops visits to family, study and work. Fees paid for
        refused Schengen visas: EUR 157.1 million in 2025 according to one collective.
        Sources: \texttt{au1994migrationregs} (F), \texttt{homeaffairs2026refunds} (F),
        \texttt{foresti2026cost} (A, advocacy).
\end{itemize}}

\teamnote{what each strand teaches us}{%
The rubric asks for what is relevant to our work, not for a list of papers.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The closest study.} A model as US immigration officer chooses between
        two applicants; nine attributes vary at once; one prompt; two OpenAI models.
        Country mattered little in the choices, but asked directly the models named
        Iraq, Sudan and Somalia for ``additional scrutiny''
        (\texttt{mao2025gatekeepers}, F). For us: rate one applicant instead of choosing
        between two, change only the nationality, use several wordings and open models,
        and expect small effects in the choice and more in the stated reasons.
  \item \textbf{LLM audits with one explicit line.} Mortgage files with a race line:
        gaps in approval and interest rate. Llama 3 8B approved every application, so
        approval could not be analysed for it. Claude 3 Opus answered only 74\% of the
        requests for Black applicants (\texttt{bowen2025measuring}, F). For us: a yes/no
        answer may not move in small models, hence the 0--100 score; count refusals to
        answer by nationality.
  \item \textbf{A gap expressed in something valuable.} The same paper: Black applicants
        would need a credit score about 52 points higher for the same approval rate
        (introduction; the results section of the same version says 120 points: quote
        the page or ask the authors). In human studies: 71 credit-score points, eight
        years of experience (\texttt{hanson2016discrimination}, A;
        \texttt{francis2003employers}, A). For us: the model for RQ4.
  \item \textbf{Direction is unstable.} Real origin labels led to fewer discriminatory
        recommendations than invented groups (\texttt{bunel2026customer}, F); in one
        study an explicit Muslim label did slightly better than other religions in loan
        approval (\texttt{arcuschin2026blind}, F). For us: expect either direction.
  \item \textbf{Explicit cues.} A stated attribute moves decisions more than a name
        (\texttt{tamkin2023evaluating}, F), but for Muslims a name triggered more than
        the word (\texttt{hemmatian2023muslim}, F). Different cues for the same group
        can give different conclusions (\texttt{tonneau2026cues}, A). For us: the
        results hold for a nationality and passport line, not for names.
  \item \textbf{Telling the model to ignore the attribute.} Brought discrimination close
        to zero in Claude 2.0 (\texttt{tamkin2023evaluating}, F) and removed the
        approval gap in mortgage files (\texttt{bowen2025measuring}, F). Instructions
        fail once realistic context is added in hiring (\texttt{karvonen2025robustly},
        F). For us: the second version of the guidance can go either way.
  \item \textbf{Poorer countries are described worse.} GPT-2 text is more negative for
        countries with fewer internet users and lower economic status
        (\texttt{venkit2023nationality}, F). For us: one reason to expect that the 22
        are not treated alike.
  \item \textbf{Real visa decisions.} Refusal rates for African countries more than
        double those for Europe, not explained by social and economic factors
        (\texttt{thomas2025global}, A, seminar abstract). Student visas: 8 percentage
        points more refusals for predominantly Muslim countries after controls
        (\texttt{maggio2026racial}, A, unpublished conference paper). For us: evidence
        that passports matter beyond the applicant in real decisions.
  \item \textbf{What a reviewer expects.} The format of an audit moves results as much
        as the group does (\texttt{vohra2026audit}, F); wording variants and an
        anti-discrimination instruction as standard (\texttt{gaebler2024auditing}, F);
        uncertainty by item, not by run (\texttt{miller2024adding}, F); models may
        notice a test (\texttt{needham2025large}, A).
  \item \textbf{Not searched systematically.} Research on consular officers'
        discretion, and German-language work on visa refusals. A few sources are in
        \texttt{research/visa\_scenario/}; find more, or say that this literature was
        not covered.
\end{itemize}}

\section{Data}
\label{sec:data}

\teamnote{materials (Estonia design; replaces the Australian notes of 2026-10-10; not report text)}{%
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Applications.} 30 invented applications (10 plain, 10 with one mild point, 10 with two) and 10 boundary applications with three mild points each (every way to choose three
        of five: new job, no travel abroad, contract ending, long stay, relative in Estonia). Everyone was born in the United Arab Emirates, has lived there since birth, is employed
        there and applies there; every application states the same legal status (``lawful long-term resident with the full right to live and work there''). The application has
        the five items of the Estonian checklist (purpose, travel medical insurance, return flight, accommodation, means of subsistence); funds are on five levels, AED 2,000 to
        10,500, of which the lowest is below the EUR 560 that 8 days at EUR 70 need; the application states funds and required funds in euros (rate AED 4.2, assumption).

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}
% Rubric [10]: Explain how you filtered data, normalized values, computed additional variables,
% etc. Detail the statistical analyses and other methods you will apply to assess your
% research question(s). Carefully document all references to data sources.

\teamnote{methods (not report text)}{%
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Outcome and effect.} The score (0 = refuse, 100 = clearly grant). For each application cell (same applicant, savings, wording and repetition) the mean over the
        nationalities is removed, so the applicant mix cancels; a nationality's effect is its mean of these deviations. Spread = standard deviation of the 21 nationality means
        that need a visa, and gap = highest minus lowest.
  \item \textbf{Confirmatory tests} (\texttt{experiment/analysis\_plan.md}, fixed before the first run; \texttt{pipeline/analyze\_main.py}). H1: spread across the 21, permutation test
        (nationality labels shuffled within application cells, 5,000 shuffles, one-sided), Holm correction over models. H2: Spearman correlation of wealth rank and nationality mean,
        same shuffles; adjusted Rand index of a three-way split. H3: change of the spread with the paragraph on nationality (paired permutation). H4: the score at AED 3,000 minus AED
        2,000, at least 10 points (does the model react to a real ground). Smallest effect of interest: 5 points.
  \item \textbf{Exploratory.} Variants (gap with one repetition against the gap that chance gives, from the same shuffles), the boundary set, the Gulf check, the cost measures
        (\texttt{pipeline/analysis/costs.py}), the judge's codes, the nine extra models, and the deep dive of 2026-11-03 to 2026-11-05 (D8): negative applicants, ablations of the
        nationality and document lines, more prompt variants, reference nationalities, the Qwen family, a judge lab, five repetitions of the key variants, conflict countries,
        thinking switched on and four other temperatures. Pair tests of two nationalities: the difference is taken inside each application cell and the unit is the applicant
        $\times$ savings pair (30 pairs), 95\% bootstrap interval, sign-flip p value (\texttt{pipeline/deep\_dive/significance\_tests.py}). Gap tests: label shuffles inside the cells
        (200 shuffles, so p cannot be below 0.005).
  \item \textbf{Changes after the plan was fixed} (\texttt{experiment/planning/deviations.md}): D1 funds in euros and ten two-point applicants after pilot 1 (99.5\% of scores at 80 or more,
        no reaction to missing funds); D2 pilot 2 missed two of four checks narrowly (91.2\% against 90\%, 9.3 against 10 points) and the main run went ahead; D3 clearer wording of one
        judge code; D4 boundary set; D5 and D7 extra models; D6 the parser reads a whole number written as text. No result by nationality was used for any of them.
  \item \textbf{Software.} Python standard library for the pipeline and the tests (235 unit tests), matplotlib for figures, vLLM 0.29 for serving, Slurm on bwUniCluster.
\end{itemize}}

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}
% Rubric [10]: Present the results of your analysis including tables and figures that
% communicate and illustrate those results.

\teamnote{results of the runs of 2026-11-02 (Estonia design; not report text)}{%
All numbers are from \texttt{results/audited\_models/} (the files \texttt{analyze\_*.txt} and the tables written by
\texttt{pipeline/analysis/summarize.py}). Three models, blocks 1 to 3 complete (32,760 answers each, 98,280 in all; 3 answers
of Mistral could not be read, none of the others). Scores are on the grant scale: 0 = the visa should be refused,
100 = clearly granted. Mean score in block 1: Qwen3 32B 86.5, Mistral Small 3.2 24B 82.6, Gemma 4 31B 79.1; 86 (Qwen),
82 (Mistral) and 73 (Gemma) per cent of the scores lie at 80 or more.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{H1, is there a spread among the 21 nationalities that need a visa (block 1)?} Yes, small. Standard
        deviation of the 21 nationality means (after removing the application's own mean) and gap between the highest and the
        lowest nationality, in score points; permutation test with 5,000 shuffles, one-sided, Holm correction over models:
        see the table. Lowest in all three models: Syria, Yemen, Sudan, Iraq, Libya, Somalia. The three placebo nationalities that need a visa
        differ by 0.2 to 0.4 points (standard deviation of their means). The differences are far below the 5 points that the analysis plan calls
        worth noting; they are real in the sense that chance does not give them.
  \item \textbf{H2, order rich, normal, poor.} Spearman correlation between wealth rank and nationality mean across the 21 countries is
        negative in all three models (table). The three-way split of the means matches the World Bank groups only weakly. Limits: the rich group
        is the five Gulf states, the poor group is four countries at war.
  \item \textbf{H3, does the paragraph ``nationality is not a ground'' help?} No. The change of the gap is within chance for all models
        (table).
  \item \textbf{H4, does the instrument work?} The score at AED 3,000 minus the score at AED 2,000 (the funds below EUR 560 for 8 days) is
        +28.4 for Gemma, +8.8 for Qwen and +2.1 for Mistral against a threshold of 10: Mistral does not use the money rule; Qwen narrowly misses.
  \item \textbf{Boundary applicants (extension D4, blocks 5 and 6, 10 applicants with three mild points each).} Gaps of 3.8 (Gemma), 3.7 (Mistral)
        and 2.4 (Qwen) points; wealth order again for Gemma and Qwen (rho $-0.69$, $-0.75$), not for Mistral ($-0.20$). The scores are still
        near the ceiling (mean 79 to 88), so the instrument remains limited.
  \item \textbf{Variants (exploratory; block 4, one repetition).} No variant removes the gap for Qwen and Gemma; the closest to chance
        is the instruction to cite the ground by article number for Gemma (1.1 points against 0.9 by chance). Figure~\ref{fig:variants}.
  \item \textbf{What the reasons say (judge OLMo 3.1 32B; not yet validated).} In block 1 about 0.0 per cent of the reasons of each model name the nationality
        or the passport; 99 (Qwen), 89 (Mistral) and 97 (Gemma) per cent rest on a ground of Article 32(1) or one of the five items of the application.
        Doubts about the right to stay in the Emirates appear in 0.1 to 0.6 per cent of block 1 reasons and in 0.1 to 3 per cent on the boundary applicants.
        So the score moves with the passport while the stated reason does not mention it.
  \item \textbf{Check without the Gulf states} (the rich group; 16 nationalities left). The spread is unchanged (gap 1.5, 2.7 and 3.4 points, $p = 0.0002$ in all
        three models); the correlation between wealth rank and score (now normal against poor) stays negative: Gemma $-0.69$, Qwen $-0.63$, Mistral $-0.38$
        ($p = 0.09$). So the order is not only the Gulf states being better scored.
  \item \textbf{What a gap costs (illustrations, \texttt{results/audited\_models/costs.csv}).} Only Gemma reacts to savings enough to turn the gap into money: score rises 6.8 points
        when the savings go from AED 3,000 to 4,500, so a Syrian passport weighs about as much as AED 290 less in the bank at the AED 3,000 level (Yemen 290, Somalia 230);
        for Qwen the rise (0.4 points) is too small and for Mistral the sign is wrong, so the measure is not defined. Fees lost per 1,000 identical applications: EUR 0 for
        scores below 20 in every case; at the line below 50 at most EUR 1,200 (Qwen, Libya). Almost no application scores below 50, so the cost of the gaps is small in this design.
  \item \textbf{Nine more models (exploratory, \texttt{experiment/planning/deviations.md} D5 and D7; 365,040 answers; same design, blocks 1 to 3, 5 and 6).}
        Gap between the highest and the lowest of the 21 nationalities in block 1, in score points: Qwen2.5 7B 29.0 (sd of the means 8.9), Mistral 7B 2.1,
        Falcon3 7B 1.8, Qwen2.5 72B 1.7, Qwen2.5 32B 1.7, gpt-oss-120b 1.6, OLMo 2 7B 1.2, Mistral Medium 3.5 128B 1.0, Phi-3.5 mini 0.0 (it answers 80 to almost
        everything); the audited models are 3.4 (Qwen3), 2.7 (Mistral Small) and 1.6 (Gemma). So the large gap belongs to one model, Qwen2.5 7B. Do not write that it shrinks with size: it is 29.0 at 7B and 1.7 at 32B and 72B, but exploratory
        runs of the same family give 2.8 to 4.2 at 0.5B to 3B and 1.0 at 14B, so only the 7B checkpoint is high (size is not the explanation). Somalia ($-21$), Syria ($-19$), Iraq ($-13$) are lowest for Qwen2.5 7B and the Gulf states about $+7$; for that model the
        paragraph on nationality \emph{raises} the spread (+6.6 points). The wealth order is negative in the small models (Falcon3 $-0.79$, Mistral 7B $-0.87$, OLMo 2 $-0.54$,
        Qwen2.5 7B $-0.76$) and absent or weak in the large ones (gpt-oss $+0.18$, Qwen2.5 72B $+0.13$, Qwen2.5 32B $-0.25$, Mistral Medium $-0.36$, $p = 0.054$).

  \item \textbf{The outlier gives a reason that the application does not support (Qwen2.5 7B, keyword count of our own, not the judge).} For applications whose funds cover
        the stay, 42 per cent of the reasons for Somali and for Syrian applicants, 39 for Iraqi and 31 for Yemeni, but 7 per cent for Qatari, Omani and Uruguayan applicants,

  \item Not done yet: validation of the judge by hand (the sample of 200 is in \texttt{experiment/judge/validation/}), the pre-run checks of Articles 21, 22 and 25a.

  \item \textbf{Which line carries the effect (ablations, one repetition).} For Qwen2.5 7B it is the line ``Travel document: Syrian passport'': with only the nationality line the gap falls from 32 to 7;

  \item \textbf{Negative applicants (twelve applications with one clear ground; blocks 7 and 8).} Where the ground is clear the passport adds little for most models (Syria minus the average of the same application:

\section{Conclusion and Discussion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% Rubric [5]: Evaluate answers to the question and their reliability. Identify
% limitations and alternative explanations for your results.

\teamnote{alternative explanations to discuss}{%
\begin{itemize}
  \item Where a difference may come from: real offices treat these nationalities
        differently, and the model has read about them. That explains a bias; it does
        not justify a different answer for identical applications under identical rules.
  \item The label may stand for the wealth, conflict or migration history of a country.
        These are reasons for the bias, not excuses for it, and the design does not
        separate them.
  \item ``Rich'' cannot be told apart from ``Gulf'': the rich group is exactly the six
        Gulf states, the same six for which the status line simplifies reality. The
        comparison of normal and poor is free of this.
  \item ``Poor'' cannot be told apart from ``at war'': Somalia, Sudan, Syria and Yemen
        are all at war or just out of one.
  \item Three groups of 6, 12 and 4 countries are small. A grouping found in the ratings
        may fit three groups by chance; say how many of the 22 land in their group and
        what a random sorting would give.
  \item The guidance names ``conditions in the home country'' and defines home country
        as the country where the applicant lives. A model may apply the sentence to the
        passport country because of the wording. A version without the sentence would
        separate this; it is not planned.
  \item The model may know more than the application says: that an Emirati is a citizen
        in the Emirates, that Gulf citizens need no visa there, that many foreign
        residents hold a visa tied to the job. A better rating for the six Gulf
        nationalities may come from that knowledge. The check without them shows how
        much of a result rests on them.
  \item A passport is not a religion. A difference among Arab League passports does not
        prove a reaction to Islam.
  \item Any set of labels spreads the scores a little. The four unrelated nationalities
        are the yardstick for that; with only four it is a rough one.
  \item Models may notice that this is a test (\texttt{vohra2026audit}, F;
        \texttt{needham2025large}, A).
  \item The applications are clean or mildly doubtful, so scores sit near the ceiling; if a model answers the same for
        everybody, the rating cannot show a difference; the score may.
  \item A result of ``no difference'' is an answer too: the models then do what the
        rules ask.
\end{itemize}}

\section*{Limitations}

\teamnote{limits to state}{%
\begin{itemize}
  \item Run on 2026-11-02 on bwUniCluster with vLLM: Qwen3 32B, Gemma 4 31B, Mistral Small 3.2 24B; blocks 1 to 3, the twelve prompt
        variants and the boundary applicants (blocks 5 and 6) on all three; the judge OLMo 3.1 32B on block 1, the three most important
        variants and block 5. Pilots: two (\texttt{experiment/planning/deviations.md} D1 to D4 lists every change after the freeze).
  \item Scores sit near the ceiling (all applications are clean or mildly doubtful): gaps are small and the instrument has little room.
        Pilot 2 missed two of its four pre-set checks by 1.2 and 0.7 points and the run went ahead (D2).
  \item Mistral does not react to the money rule; its other results are read with that in mind.
  \item The judge has not yet been validated against a hand-coded sample; its codes are exploratory.
  \item Not read in full text: Articles 14, 15, 21, 22 and 25a of the Visa Code; the visa exemption of the Emirates and Uruguay.
        Not confirmed: that Estonia uses AI for visa decisions.
  \item No authority is documented as using a language model to rate visa applications.
        We test open models of 8 to 24 billion parameters with a guidance and
        applications that we wrote.
  \item The yardstick is the published rules, read in part by non-lawyers; the
        instruction it draws on is from 2016. Rules and bans change: the table refers to
        10 October 2026.
  \item One destination, one visa, one country of residence, English only.
  \item Synthetic applications without name and gender; nationality given as two
        explicit lines; applicants born and living in the United Arab Emirates, which is
        common for some of the nationalities and rare for others; invented incomes.
  \item One legal status line for everybody simplifies reality: an Emirati is a citizen
        in the Emirates, Gulf citizens need no visa there, and a long-term visa that
        needs no sponsor is not what most residents hold.
  \item The facts about the Emirates come from an abstract of the nationality law, three
        government web pages and one press report of official figures.
  \item The cost figures are illustrations, not forecasts.
  \item The deep dive (D8) is exploratory and was not pre-registered. Most of its cells have one repetition (90 to 450 answers per cell); tests on those have wide chance ranges, and the shuffle test cannot give
        p below 0.005. The reference and conflict nationalities (German, American, Indian, Filipino, Pakistani, Afghan, Ukrainian, Eritrean, Burmese, Nepalese, Vietnamese, Venezuelan) were added at the project lead's request
        and are a baseline for how a model reads a passport, not realistic applicants; the project is not a comparison of Arab with non-Arab countries.
  \item The judge lab shows agreement between judges, not correctness; the 200 reasons for hand validation are still uncoded.
  \item The thinking runs show the thinking of Qwen3 and gpt-oss only; a reasoning text is not a faithful account of why a model scored as it did.
\end{itemize}}
